The proposed fee vault

Status: planned treasury infrastructure. No deployed vault or automated strategy is claimed.

The first vault is intended to give project fees a dedicated home and a readable record of how they are used. Its job is to receive funds, preserve an operating balance, and support approved product spending and buybacks.

How it would work

  1. Creator fees are claimed to the designated recipient, then received by the treasury. Direct receipt depends on what the launch platform supports.
  2. Each receipt is reconciled against its transaction and recorded once.
  3. A published budget assigns available funds to operations, , buybacks, or retention.
  4. Completed spending and purchases are recorded with their evidence.
  5. Unspent amounts carry forward. Any later allocation change is recorded.

The vault address, the people or keys controlling spending, and any contract or withdrawal powers will be disclosed before it is presented as active. A public balance helps visibility; it does not by itself impose spending restrictions.

Does it compound?

Retaining fees builds a balance. Spending some of those fees on a useful product may help generate future income. That is a reinvestment loop, and its success depends on actual demand and costs.

Yield compounding would require a separate income-generating strategy whose returns are reinvested. There is no such strategy in the first vault plan. Funds spent buying and burning tokens are spent; those same funds cannot also remain invested in the vault.

The project will therefore measure receipts, operating costs, runway, and delivered utility before describing the system as self-funding. No APY or automatic treasury growth is promised.

What the vault means for holders

The vault is planned to manage project revenue. It is not a public deposit product, a holder redemption pool, or a mechanism that gives the token ownership of treasury assets.
